## Key Concepts

### Governance

Governance establishes how cybersecurity is managed within an organization.

It includes areas such as:

- Security policies and procedures
- Roles and responsibilities
- Access management policies
- Risk management processes
- Organizational security objectives

Governance helps ensure that security decisions support both cybersecurity requirements and business objectives.

### Risk Management

Risk management involves identifying assets, threats, vulnerabilities, and potential impacts in order to understand and reduce cybersecurity risk.

A basic risk assessment process includes:

1. Identify assets
2. Identify potential threats and vulnerabilities
3. Evaluate existing security controls
4. Determine likelihood and potential impact
5. Identify security gaps
6. Recommend appropriate controls or remediation

### Compliance

Compliance focuses on meeting applicable security standards, regulations, and industry requirements.

Frameworks and compliance requirements encountered in my projects include:

- NIST Cybersecurity Framework (NIST CSF)
- Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS)
- General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)
- System and Organization Controls (SOC)

## Security Controls

Security controls are safeguards used to reduce cybersecurity risks.

The controls examined in this section can be grouped into three major categories:

### Administrative / Managerial Controls

Examples:

- Least privilege
- Separation of duties
- Password policies
- Access control policies
- Disaster recovery planning

### Technical Controls

Examples:

- Firewalls
- Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS)
- Encryption
- Antivirus software
- Backups
- Password management systems

### Physical / Operational Controls

Examples:

- Physical locks
- CCTV surveillance
- Fire detection and prevention systems

Controls can also serve different purposes, including:

- Preventative
- Detective
- Corrective
- Deterrent

Multiple controls can work together to provide defense in depth.

## Key Takeaways

One of the most important lessons I learned while performing security control assessments is that the existence of a security control does not necessarily mean that the control is adequate or effective.

A security assessment should consider not only:

> **Does the control exist?**

but also:

> **Is the control properly implemented, sufficiently restrictive, consistently enforced, and effective at addressing the identified risk?**

For example, an organization may have a password policy, but the control may still be inadequate if its requirements are too weak. Similarly, maintaining an inventory of organizational assets does not necessarily mean those assets have been properly classified.

This distinction helped me move from simply identifying security controls to evaluating their effectiveness and adequacy within an organization's overall security posture.
